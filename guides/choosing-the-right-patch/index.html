Guides · Choosing the technique

How to choose the right patch

Embroidered, Woven HD, PVC or sublimated: the correct technique is not an aesthetic preference, it is a consequence of the logo, the intended use and the quantity. This guide explains how to reach the decision before production, not after.

The four variables that determine the choice

Almost every question about which patch to choose is answered by looking at four things. None of them is a matter of personal taste: they are technical constraints, and on their own they define which techniques remain viable.

Four questions to answer first

  • How is the logo built? — solid colours and clean shapes, or thin lines, small text, gradients and shading
  • How large will the patch be? — the size in millimetres decides what level of detail is physically reproducible
  • Where will it be used? — a frequently washed uniform, a technical garment, outdoor equipment, an event giveaway
  • How many pieces, and will there be more? — a one-off run and a recurring reorder lead to different decisions

If you don't have all the answers yet, that's fine: two out of four are often enough to narrow the choice. Tell us what you know and we will work through the rest together.

The main constraint

The logo decides more than preference does

Every technique has a detail threshold below which the result gets worse rather than better. It is that threshold, not aesthetics, that rules a technique out.

Solid colours, clean shapes

A crest with three or four solid colours and clear outlines is the ideal case for embroidery: each colour becomes an area of stitches, and the result has depth and perceived quality.

Thin lines, small text

If the logo contains fine strokes, characters below 4–5 mm or many closely spaced details, embroidery loses them. Woven HD exists for this: the weave uses finer threads and holds the detail.

Gradients and photographs

Gradients, shadows, continuous transitions and photographic images are not embroidered, they are printed. Sublimation reproduces the full colour range with no limit on the number of colours.

!

A logo designed for print is not ready for a patch

A logo designed for print or screen often contains elements that become unreadable at 60 mm wide on fabric: straplines, taglines, thin frames, founding dates. In those cases you don't scale everything down proportionally — you simplify the artwork for the real size, keeping recognisable what actually matters.

The four techniques compared

For each one: how it is made, when it is the correct choice and — above all — when it is not. The limits declared here are the same ones you will find on the dedicated technique pages.

Embroidered patches

Thread stitched point by point onto a twill or felt base, following a machine file. Raised surface, institutional look, high resistance to washing.

  • StrengthPerceived quality and durability
  • Minimum detailText generally legible from 4–5 mm
  • Choose it ifSolid-colour logo, use on uniforms
  • Avoid it ifThe logo has gradients or micro-detail

Woven HD patches

High-definition weaving: the design is formed within the interlacing threads, not on top of the fabric. Flat, thin surface with much finer detail than embroidery.

  • StrengthFine detail, legible small text
  • ThicknessFlat: suited to lightweight garments
  • Choose it ifComplex logos, thin lines, small text
  • Avoid it ifYou need the raised effect of embroidery

PVC patches

Moulded soft rubber with solid colours and a sculpted, raised surface. Waterproof and resistant to abrasion and the elements.

  • StrengthResistance to water, wear and outdoor use
  • FinishSolid colours, three-dimensional relief
  • Choose it ifTactical gear, outdoor, heavy use
  • Avoid it ifA textile or institutional look is required

Sublimated patches

Artwork printed by sublimation onto a textile base: the ink enters the fibre rather than sitting on the surface. No limit on the number of colours.

  • StrengthGradients, photographs, many colours
  • Run sizeViable on small quantities too
  • Choose it ifComplex artwork, events, merchandising

Guided choice

If this is your case, start here

Typical starting points, not absolute rules: a specific project can lead somewhere else, and we say so during assessment.

Institutional or operational uniforms

Official crest, frequent washing, the need to reorder identically for years: embroidery with a sew-on backing, or hook-and-loop if rank or unit must be interchangeable.

Sports club or football academy

Logos with plenty of detail and small text on lightweight garments: Woven HD. If the crest is simple and a raised textile effect is wanted, embroidery still works.

Scouts and associations

Badges sewn onto uniforms and backpacks, daily use and repeated washing: embroidery with a sew-on backing. It has the longest service life.

Motorcycle clubs, airsoft, tactical use

Exposure to rain, rubbing and dirt: PVC with a hook-and-loop backing. Embroidery is possible but wears out sooner in extreme conditions.

Events, giveaways, merchandising

Colour-rich artwork, limited quantity, short lead times: sublimation. The unit cost stays reasonable without large volumes.

Corporate workwear

It depends on the logo: solid colours → embroidery; a mark with fine lettering → Woven HD. The garment fabric then determines the backing type.

Application

The backing: how the patch reaches the garment

The backing must be decided before production. Changing it afterwards requires additional work, and in some cases is not possible at all: it is the decision most often postponed and most often the source of problems.

Sew-on

Textile backing, sewn by hand or machine. It is the most durable option over time and the reference choice for daily-use uniforms and frequently washed garments.

Iron-on

Applied with heat using a press or an iron. The result depends on the fabric and the temperature the garment tolerates: on technical or heat-sensitive fabrics, sewing remains more reliable. Read more.

Hook only

Only the hook side goes on the patch. The matching loop panel is needed on the garment: if the garment is not prepared, it has to be applied separately. Plan this before ordering. Read more.

Hook + loop together

The supply includes both the hook on the back of the patch and the loop panel to be applied to the garment. Useful when the garments are not already prepared for hook-and-loop.

Border and shape: a technical choice, not a decorative one

The border is not an optional finish: it is part of the patch and depends on the shape. The two most common finishes on embroidery behave differently.

  • Merrow border — the cord that wraps around the perimeter. A tidy, recognisable finish, but it only works well on simple shapes: circles, ovals, regular shields.
  • Laser cut — the outline is cut following the contour. It handles irregular shapes, corners and intricate silhouettes where a merrow cannot turn cleanly.

Both finishes are produced through selected external manufacturing and generally become economically worthwhile from around 200 pieces: below that threshold we assess the most sensible solution case by case rather than applying a fixed rule.

The practical rule: the shape of the logo determines the border, not the other way round. If the desired border is incompatible with the silhouette, it is the silhouette that should be reconsidered.

Artwork and files

Preparing the artwork

The quality of the starting file affects lead times, cost and fidelity more than any other variable the customer controls.

What makes a file ready

  • Vector format — AI, EPS, PDF or SVG. Failing that, a raster at 300 dpi or more at real size
  • Outlined text — converted to curves, so the font cannot change on another computer
  • Declared colours — Pantone references or precise indications wherever colour is binding
  • Real size stated — width and height in millimetres, not "about logo size"
  • A contained number of colours — 3–5 colours translate better into embroidery and shorten production

No vector file? A photograph or a scan is a valid starting point: we will tell you whether it is sufficient or whether the artwork needs rebuilding, before we start.

i

Digitising happens only once

In embroidery, the logo is converted into a machine file: that is digitising, and it determines stitch density, thread direction and colour changes. Done well, it stays valid forever: every future reorder starts from it, identical to the original, without repeating the initial work. How it is archived.

PatchLab Academy

Five mistakes we see repeated

None of them comes from carelessness: they come from information nobody had provided before ordering.

Mistake 1

Choosing the technique before looking at the logo

"We want embroidered patches" is a preference, not a specification. If the logo has 2 mm text or a gradient, embroidery will return something different from the approved file — and that surfaces on the sample, after time has already been spent.

Mistake 2

Ordering hook-and-loop without checking the garment

The hook side on the patch needs the loop panel on the garment. If the uniforms are not prepared, the patches arrive correct but unusable until the fabric is worked on: an unplanned cost and delay.

Mistake 3

Scaling down instead of simplifying

Taking a print logo to 55 mm while keeping every element means making half the artwork illegible. The correct solution is a version dedicated to the real size, preserving what makes the mark recognisable.

Mistake 4

Deciding the backing at the last minute

Sew-on, iron-on and hook-and-loop are not interchangeable once production has started. The backing should be chosen together with the technique, considering the garment fabric and who will apply the patches.

Mistake 5

Not thinking about the second order

If the machine file, colours and approved sample are not archived, a reorder twelve months later costs like a first order. Asking a supplier how specifications are stored is a legitimate question.

Frequently asked

Questions about choosing the technique

  • What is the practical difference between embroidered and woven patches?

    Embroidery adds thread on top of a base fabric: the result has thickness and relief. Weaving creates the design inside the interlacing: the result is flat and thin, but holds much finer detail. In short: embroidery for a tactile, institutional effect; woven when the logo has thin lines or small text that embroidery would lose.

  • What is the minimum size for a patch with text?

    In embroidery, a logo with legible text generally starts at around 50×30 mm, and letters stay legible from 4–5 mm high depending on the typeface and the background. Below those thresholds detail degrades. With Woven HD you can go smaller, because weaving handles finer strokes.

  • Does the number of colours change the cost?

    In embroidery yes: every colour change requires an operation in production, so 3–5 colour logos are more efficient than artwork with many transitions. In sublimation the number of colours is not a constraint, because the artwork is printed. The final cost still depends on technique, size, border, backing and quantity together: it is assessed per project.

  • Can I mix different techniques in the same order?

    Yes, and it is a common choice: for example an embroidered main crest for the official uniform and a woven version for lightweight technical garments. Each technique keeps its own lead times and minimums, so the supply has to be planned around both.

  • How can I be sure of the result before production?

    Through the confirmation sample: before the production run we make one real piece for you to approve. That is the moment when colours, dimensions, detail rendering and backing type are verified — not afterwards. The full process step by step.

  • What if I chose the wrong technique?

    If our assessment shows the requested technique is not suited to the logo or the intended use, we say so and propose the alternative, explaining why. We do not produce a patch we know will fall short of expectations: it becomes a complaint, not a customer who reorders.
